1. Executive decision and proposal

The opportunity is not simply to sell lower-cost labour. It is to make it easier for U.S. property-management companies to delegate repeatable back-office work without losing control of their systems, approvals, data or service standards.

Proposed business

Ferrari Capital Management

Recruit, prepare and support offshore specialists for U.S. property-management operations. Start with narrowly scoped workflows, transparent access rules and a measurable service promise. Expand only after delivery quality, client acceptance and unit economics are demonstrated.

Proposed positioning: Property operations support with clear accountability, controlled access and predictable delivery.

Recommended path

Validate first, then standardize

  1. Interview prospective U.S. clients about tasks they will outsource and access restrictions.
  2. Test both service models with one or two design-partner clients.
  3. Confirm the true cost of devices, software, supervision, payroll and client acquisition.
  4. Publish a clear service menu, security boundary and pricing schedule.
  5. Scale only after repeatable onboarding and quality controls exist.

2. Market positioning and competitive lesson

Established providers illustrate two broad patterns. Managed providers bundle recruiting, training, workforce support, security controls and performance oversight. Marketplace or client-managed arrangements give the client more direct control of hiring, access and day-to-day supervision, with optional support services. These are useful reference models, not proof that all U.S. companies accept offshore access from Nigeria.

Managed-service reference

Revaya-style positioning

  • Dedicated staffing and property-management workflow familiarity.
  • Structured onboarding, HR/performance support and documented controls.
  • Potentially higher fee because the provider takes on more coordination and oversight.
  • FCM lesson: only promise controls and replacement support that are implemented and budgeted.
Client-managed reference

Marketplace / direct-management positioning

  • Client retains direct daily management and often sets permissions and workflow priorities.
  • Provider focuses on sourcing, screening, payment or workforce tools.
  • Lower ongoing provider workload, but more work and risk remain with the client.
  • FCM lesson: clearly state who supervises, approves work, manages access and handles incidents.
Competitive boundary: any market pricing discussed during planning is indicative and may change. FCM should not rely on competitor price claims until current public pricing and package inclusions are rechecked. 3. Two service models: managed versus client-managed

These offers must not be sold as the same service at different prices. The client-managed model reduces FCM's supervision burden but should also reduce the promises FCM makes. It does not remove the need for confidentiality, secure access, lawful contracting or a clear incident process.

Model A · Recommended core offer

Managed specialist

FCM owns: recruiting and screening, role-fit checks, onboarding, training, defined work standards, routine QA, attendance coordination, performance check-ins, security-policy administration within agreed scope, escalation tracking and documented handover.

Client owns: business priorities, system and data authorization, final decisions, approvals, payment release, tenant/owner policy decisions, legal determinations and any licensed activity.

Best for: clients who want the specialist to be productive without having to manage every administrative detail themselves.

One-time placement and onboarding fee: $950 per successful placement as the proposed launch price. Covers recruitment, screening, client selection support, role-specific onboarding, workflow documentation and initial quality checks.

Monthly managed service fee: $1,850–$2,000 for a full-time 160-hour placement, subject to cost and demand validation.

Model B · Lower-touch offer

Client-managed specialist

FCM owns: sourcing, screening, placement, onboarding, payroll and specialist payments, timesheet logging and submission for client approval, payment administration, a monthly quality scorecard, and defined payroll/attendance issue resolution.

Client owns: daily supervision, task assignment, training on its internal processes, day-to-day work review, timely approval or dispute of submitted timesheets, system permissions and business/operational decisions. FCM pays the specialist in both models under the agreed payroll schedule, using the approved hours and documented dispute process.

Best for: experienced clients with documented SOPs, an internal supervisor and the capacity to manage a remote team member.

One-time placement fee: $1,200 per successful placement as the proposed launch price. Covers sourcing, screening, candidate introduction, client selection support and basic onboarding.

Monthly client-managed service fee: proposed at $1,650 per full-time placement. This includes payroll administration and payment, timesheet logging and submission for client approval before payroll processing, a monthly quality scorecard, and defined administrative support. The client manages day-to-day work; FCM still administers and pays its specialists in both service models.

Unmanaged does not mean uncontrolled

Even where the client supervises the specialist, FCM should maintain a minimum standard: signed confidentiality terms, documented scope, a defined access/incident escalation procedure, proof of onboarding and a clean offboarding checklist. The distinction is who manages day-to-day output and system administration, not whether basic safeguards exist.

Recommended price guardrails

  • Do not discount the managed service below fully loaded direct cost plus a contribution sufficient to fund central operations.
  • Set a minimum monthly commitment or scope cap; open-ended work creates unpriced supervision.
  • Quote part-time roles based on actual hours and minimum delivery costs, not a straight fraction of full-time pricing.

  • Review pricing quarterly during the first year as actual costs and retention data emerge.

Tooling approach

Evaluate the client's existing tools first. Potential building blocks may include device management, endpoint protection, MFA, password management, secure email, access logs and remote support. Product names such as Microsoft Intune, Defender for Business or Entra ID are options to assess, not assumed purchases or claims of compliance. Check licensing, device compatibility, data location, administrator capacity and total cost before selecting.

Do not advertise “enterprise-grade security,” “fully compliant,” “24/7 monitoring” or a formal certification unless the capability is real, appropriately scoped and supported by evidence. Security questionnaires and legal terms may require professional review.

7. Operating model: from lead to stable delivery

Step 1 · Client qualification

Confirm portfolio type and size, software, recurring task volume, U.S. business-hour overlap, data categories, offshore-access policy, existing SOPs, supervisor availability, security requirements, budget and success measures. Reject opportunities that require prohibited access or unclear responsibility.

Step 2 · Scope and proposal

Define role outcomes, hours, working schedule, task queue, communication channels, turnaround times, acceptance criteria, exclusions, escalation levels, equipment owner, system owner, pricing, payment timing, term, termination and replacement terms.

Step 3 · Candidate recruitment and assessment

Screen written communication, spreadsheet accuracy, attention to detail, workflow judgment, confidentiality awareness, reliability and role-specific software familiarity. Use a practical test with fictional or sanitized data. Verify references and identity only through lawful, proportionate processes.

Step 4 · Contracting and security approval

Complete the client vendor/security review, contract and confidentiality documents, data-processing terms where applicable, device choice, account request, access matrix, approved location and incident contacts before any production access.

Step 5 · Onboarding and controlled pilot

Train on the client's SOPs, use sandbox or redacted records where possible, run a limited task batch, review errors daily during the initial period and document client sign-off before expanding the task scope.

Step 6 · Business-as-usual delivery

Daily task queue and exception log; weekly client update; regular sample-based QA; monthly KPI review; access review after role changes; documented coaching and retraining when issues recur.

Step 7 · Renewal, expansion or offboarding

Review workload, quality, value, security changes and economics. Expand permissions only with client approval. On exit, transfer open work, return assets, revoke access and confirm data disposition.

12. Assumptions, definitions and validation notes

Definitions

  • Managed specialist: FCM provides defined ongoing oversight and quality controls in addition to staffing. Client retains business authority and system authorization.
  • Client-managed specialist: client handles daily task management and internal workflow training; FCM still administers payroll, pays the specialist, logs and submits timesheets for client approval, and provides the agreed quality scorecard.
  • Direct delivery costs: costs that vary with a placement, such as equipment allocation, security tools, onboarding, QA and payment administration.
  • Central overhead: shared costs such as general administration, marketing, legal/accounting, management and business systems.
  • Contribution before central overhead: client revenue minus specialist compensation and direct delivery costs. It is not net profit.
  • Residual / surplus: contribution less the assumed central overhead; still not net profit if other costs or founder compensation are omitted.
